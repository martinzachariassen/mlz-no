Release Train

Turning a fortnightly release window that four people had to be awake for into a progressive rollout nobody has to attend.

Releases went out every second Thursday evening, by hand, with a rollback plan that was a document rather than a command. I replaced the window with a pipeline that ships continuously through a canary and a ramp, and rolls itself back when the error budget starts burning.

Problem

The window was the risk

A fortnightly window meant a fortnight of changes went out at once, which meant that when something broke there were sixty commits to bisect and four people on a call at nine in the evening doing it. The window existed to make releases safe and was the single largest thing making them dangerous.

What the window was really buying

  • A moment when everyone who might be needed was already awake.
  • A rollback story: redeploy the previous tag, by hand, and hope nothing had migrated.
  • A batch large enough that nobody could say which change caused which graph to move.

Approach

Ship small, watch, and let it undo itself

The pipeline takes a merged commit to one percent of traffic, holds it there while a fixed set of signals is compared against the incumbent, then ramps. Nothing about that needs a human: the decision at each gate is arithmetic on the error budget, and the interesting outcome — stop and revert — is the one that happens automatically.

on_breach: rollback, not pause

The first version paused and paged, because reverting automatically felt reckless. In practice the page arrived, a sleepy person read a dashboard for four minutes, and then reverted anyway. Reverting first and paging afterwards has never once been the wrong call, and it turned a four-minute decision into a ninety-second one.

Result

Nobody attends a release any more

Thirty-one hours sounds slow for continuous delivery, and it is — deliberately. Most of it is the canary hold, which is the part that does the work. The change that matters is not the number but that it is the same number every time, and that nobody has to be present for any of it.

What I would do differently

  • Make database migrations a first-class stage from the start. Treating them as "just another deploy" cost us the only rollback the pipeline could not perform.
  • Agree the comparison metrics with the teams before building the gates, not after. Two services needed a signal we had not thought to collect.
